# Printing and assembling the penguin

Everything in this folder is generated by [`../generate.py`](../generate.py)
and committed, so you don't need to run anything to print it.

It's a penguin built on a **10 mm voxel grid** — nothing is rounded, nothing
is filleted. A black shell, a white belly that lights up as a block of
pixels, and two cubes for feet. Assembled it's **80 × 60 × 120 mm** — it
stands next to a monitor without getting in the way.

**Five printed parts. Four screws. Four heat-set inserts. No supports
anywhere.**

---

## What's in here

| File | Filament | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| `body.stl` | **Black** | The penguin. Head, flippers, feet, and the window the belly fills. Hollow, open at the back. |
| `chassis.stl` | **White** | The belly *and* the carrier. Its front face is what glows; the LEDs, the dev board and the loom all mount inside it. |
| `back.stl` | **Black** | Rear cover. Four screws, and the pillars that press the chassis home. |
| `eyes.stl` | **White** | Two plugs on a bar, pupils cut right through. One part, both eyes. |
| `beak.stl` | **Black** (or orange) | A blunt wedge with a tang. Press fit. |

### The plates

The `.3mf` files are pre-arranged plates. Open one in Bambu Studio and it
arrives laid out and correctly oriented — **don't rotate anything.**

| Plate | Filament | Parts | Rough time |
|---|---|---|---|
| `plate1_black.3mf` | Black | `body`, `back`, `beak` | ~9 h |
| `plate2_white.3mf` | White | `chassis`, `eyes` | ~3 h |

Two plates, one filament change, done. Everything fits a **Bambu A1 mini**
(180 × 180 × 180); the largest part is the body at 80 × 120 × 60 mm. On a
256 mm machine, `python3 generate.py --plate 256` re-packs the same set.

### Print settings

| | |
|---|---|
| Layer height | 0.2 mm |
| Walls | 3 |
| Infill | 15% |
| Supports | **None.** Not on any part. |
| Brim | Not needed — every part has a large flat face on the plate |

Nothing overhangs past 45°. That is checked, not assumed: `generate.py`
measures the downward-facing area of every part in its print orientation and
fails if any of it is unsupported. The one ceiling in the build is the LED
shelf inside the chassis, which bridges 37 mm between walls — a bridge, not
an overhang, and 37 mm bridges print clean.

> **The chassis must be white or natural filament.** Its front face is the
> only thing between the LEDs and you. A dark filament gives you a dark
> rectangle.

Print the `eyes` in natural or clear if you have it — they pick up spill
light from inside and glow faintly.

**Filament:** about 105 g black and 44 g white for one complete penguin.

## Assembly

The whole electrical build happens on the chassis, on the bench, with
nothing in the way. Then it drops into the body in one move.

### 0. Get the electronics working first

On a breadboard, before anything is soldered short:

```bash
cd firmware && pio run -e esp32s3 -t upload
cd ../host && pip install -e . && rookery test
```

On boot the firmware walks all six LEDs in order. Any LED that stays dark is
a wiring fault, and you want to find it now rather than after it's inside a
penguin. Full circuit in [`../WIRING.md`](../WIRING.md).

### 1. Four heat-set inserts, into the body

Lay the body face down, open back up. Four round bosses sit against the
inside wall — two at shoulder height, two low. Their pockets face straight
up at you.

Set the iron to about **200 °C** for PLA, sit the insert on the hole, and let
the iron's own weight carry it in. The pockets have a 45° lead-in, so a
straight start is easy. Pushing hard squeezes molten plastic up around the
flange and leaves the insert proud. Stop when the top is flush and let it
cool before touching it.

The pockets are cut **3.85 mm** across for a **4.0 mm** insert. That 0.15 mm
is deliberate — it's the material the insert melts into and grips. If yours
measure differently, `python3 generate.py --insert-od 4.2` and reprint.

### 2. Load the LEDs into the chassis

Six holes in the shelf, a third of the way down the box. Push each LED in
**from the open back, dome first**, until the little flange at its base
catches on the shelf. That sets the height for you: the domes end up 14.4 mm
from the inside of the belly, and nothing sticks out the back to foul the
board.

Keep the colour order consistent so you can trace the loom later.

> **Worth doing:** rub the dome of each LED flat on 400-grit sandpaper until
> it's frosted. A clear 5 mm LED throws a narrow ~20° beam and puts a bright
> spot in the middle of the belly; a sanded one scatters across a much wider
> angle and lights the whole panel evenly. Two minutes, no cost, and it is
> the single biggest improvement you can make to how this looks.

Bend all six cathodes together into a ring under the shelf and solder —
that's your ground bus. Solder each anode to its resistor, then a wire from
each resistor back toward the board. **Leave the wires long.** The gap down
one side of the shelf is the route for them.

### 3. Clip the dev board in

Four posts stand on the back of the shelf. Press the board down onto them
and it clicks under four retention tabs — columns just outboard of the
board with a lip that reaches back over its edge. It sits **upright, USB
connector pointing down**, with 3.5 mm of clearance in front of it for the
LED tails.

The board is **longer than the box is tall**, on purpose. Its top passes out
through a slot in the chassis roof into the head, and its USB connector sits
low enough that a plug has **22.9 mm of clear drop** below it — enough for a
normal straight USB-C plug, which a box sized to the window could never have
given you. A second slot in the chassis floor is what the plug passes
through.

Solder the LED loom to the board per the channel map in
[`../WIRING.md`](../WIRING.md).

The chassis is now the whole electrical assembly: one object, on your bench,
that you can pick up and test.

### 4. Eyes and beak

Press the **eyes** into the head from **inside** the body — the bar behind
them stops them going out the front. The **beak** goes in from the front;
its tang presses into the socket below the eyes.

Both are 0.18 mm interference fits. A dab of glue if you want to be sure.

### 5. Thread the cable

Feed the USB cable through the slot in the bottom of the back cover
**before** plugging it into the board, then zip-tie it to the two posts
either side of the slot. A tug on the cable then pulls on the enclosure and
not on the board's USB connector.

### 6. Close it up

Drop the chassis into the body from behind. Its 45° flare seats against the
back of the window and the face comes out flush with the black skin — it
cannot go forward through the window, and the pocket it sits in locates it
sideways.

Then the back cover. The four pillars on it land on the chassis's rear rim
and press it into its seat, which is what takes the rattle out. **4 × M3 ×
1/4"** through into the body's inserts. Snug, not tight — you're clamping a
printed part.

Rubber feet underneath if you have them.

## Choosing the face

The belly is plain by default: 4 × 6 lit pixels, the same voxel grid as the
body carried across the glowing panel. That's the version that looks most
like a finished product, and it's what `plate2_white.3mf` contains.

You can cut a glyph into it instead:

```bash
python3 generate.py --face smiley      # a smiley face
python3 generate.py --face chick       # a pixel penguin chick
python3 generate.py --face text --text "BUSY"
```

The glyph is thinned from the front to a 0.9 mm floor, so it glows brighter
than the panel around it.

**This is a print-time choice, not a swap.** The belly is the chassis now —
it carries the board and the LEDs — so changing the face means printing
another chassis and moving the electronics across. That's the trade for
having one part do both jobs and for the build only needing four screws. The
body and the back cover never change, so it's one 44 g print rather than a
rebuild.

## Regenerating

`stl/` is committed, so you can print without running anything. To change
dimensions:

```bash
pip install trimesh manifold3d shapely numpy
cd hardware
python3 generate.py                    # rewrites stl/
python3 generate.py --insert-od 4.2    # if your inserts are fatter
python3 generate.py --led-d 3.0        # 3 mm LEDs instead of 5 mm
python3 generate.py --leds ring        # one WS2812B ring instead of six LEDs
python3 generate.py --plate 256        # A1 / P1 / X1
```

**The shape is a pixel map you can edit.** `PIXELS` at the top of
`generate.py` is the penguin, one character per voxel:

```python
PIXELS = [
    "..####..",   # 0   crown
    ".######.",   # 1   eyes
    ".######.",   # 2   beak
    ".######.",   # 3   neck
    "########",   # 4   shoulders, window top
    "########",   # 5   flippers
    ...
```

Change it and the whole enclosure follows — the window, the eyes, the beak
and the feet are all addressed by grid coordinates, and the engraved pixel
grid regenerates to match. `PITCH` is the voxel size.

Every run checks itself and exits non-zero if anything is wrong:

- every part is manifold **after welding at the precision the file is
  written at** — a mesh that only holds together at full float precision
  will fail in someone else's slicer
- every part fits the plate
- **every part is a single connected lump of plastic** — anything else means
  a feature is floating in mid-air, attached to nothing, which a slicer will
  happily print as a blob on the plate
- no part has unsupported downward-facing area
- a solid standing in for a USB-C plug actually reaches the connector
- the LED shelf's bridge span is measured
- the screw arithmetic — thread engagement and clearance past the tip
- the LEDs clear the dev board
- the assembled parts are boolean-intersected against each other, and
  against a solid standing in for the dev board, so a clash is a failure
  rather than a surprise at the bench

The two numbers you're most likely to need to change are `INSERT_OD`
(measure your inserts) and `BOARD_L` / `BOARD_W` (measure your board).
